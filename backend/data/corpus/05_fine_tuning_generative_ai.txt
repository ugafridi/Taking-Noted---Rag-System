2. What Fine-Tuning Is
Fine-tuning means taking a pre-trained model and continuing its training on your own labeled
examples. You do not start from zero. You start from a model that already understands
language, and you teach it your task.
Think of a readymade suit. It fits most people acceptably, the way you used DeepSeek as-is
through prompting in Week 3 and got decent answers. Fine-tuning is taking that suit to a tailor:
small adjustments, and now it fits you. Training from scratch would be weaving the fabric
yourself: possible, but slow and expensive, and unnecessary when good cloth already exists.

Fine-tuning in one picture: a pre-trained model plus your labeled data, adjusted with a short training run.

Three ingredients, nothing more: (1) a pre-trained model, (2) your labeled examples in the
format of the task, (3) a short training run with a small learning rate. Tomorrow you will run all
three on Kaggle.

3. What Actually Changes Inside
A model’s skill lives in its weights: millions of numbers that decide how it reacts to text. Pretraining set those numbers using huge amounts of text. Fine-tuning nudges them, a little, in the
direction of your examples.

Small steps on purpose
Two settings make fine-tuning gentle. First, the learning rate is small: each training step
changes the weights only slightly, so the model adjusts without forgetting what it knew.
Second, you train for few epochs. An epoch is one full pass over your data, and a handful of
passes is usually enough.
The danger of big steps: if you retrain hard on a small dataset, the model memorizes your few
dozen examples and forgets general language. Practitioners call this overfitting, and in the
extreme case catastrophic forgetting. The plain version: shouting a few dozen sentences at a
student until they forget everything else they knew.

Training from scratch needs far more of everything. Fine-tuning is the small bar in each row.

The Whole Trick
Fine-tuning works because the model already knows the language. You are only teaching it
your accent. That is why a small dataset can be enough.

5. Generative vs Discriminative AI
In Week 5 you measured a discriminative model: the Titanic classifier sorts passengers into
boxes, survived or not survived. The output is always one of a fixed set of labels. Spam or not
spam works the same way.
Generative models do the opposite job: they create new things. A paragraph that did not exist
before. An image from a description. An answer to a question. DeepSeek, which you prompted
in Week 3, belongs to this family.

Two families. Discriminative models choose a label. Generative models produce new text.

The two families are not rivals. A shop could use a classifier to route complaints and a
generator to draft replies. This week you will fine-tune a model yourself, and the ideas transfer
across both families: generative models are fine-tuned the same way.

6. How a Language Model Writes Text
A language model writes the way you might finish someone’s sentence: it looks at the words so
far and guesses the most likely next word. Then it adds that word and guesses again. One word
at a time, until it stops.
The unit it predicts is called a token. A token is usually a whole word, sometimes a piece of one,
like "un" and "happy". For this guide, thinking "word" is close enough.

The model scores every candidate and usually picks a likely one. Same sentence as your Week 3 tokenizer demo.

Two consequences worth remembering. First, the model has no plan for the whole paragraph.
It only ever picks the next word. Coherent essays emerge from thousands of good local
guesses. Second, because it picks by probability, the same prompt can give different answers
on different runs. That is a feature for creativity and a hazard for facts.

7. Prompting vs Fine-Tuning
For a generative model there are two ways to change its behavior. Prompting means putting
instructions and examples inside the input. In Week 3 you did this with a system prompt ("You
are a helpful assistant. Keep answers short.") plus user messages, sent through
chat_completion. The model itself does not change. Fine-tuning means training the model on
many examples so the behavior is baked into its weights.

8. What Data Fine-Tuning Needs
Fine-tuning data is a list of input-output pairs in the exact format of the task. To fine-tune a
generative model like DeepSeek for polite customer replies, each pair is one customer
message and the ideal reply:

Input

Output

Reply politely to this customer: Where is my order?

Your order is on its way and
should reach you in two days.
Thank you for your patience.

Reply politely to this customer: The price on the shelf was different.

You are right to point that out.
We will charge the shelf price
and fix the tag today.

Quality beats quantity. Sixty clean, consistent pairs teach more than six hundred sloppy ones.
Labeling disagreements are the enemy: if two people write different ideal replies for the same
message, the model learns confusion.
Your dataset will be small, and that is the point of this week’s demo. Fine-tuning works with
small data precisely because the model already knows the language. You are teaching it your
accent, not the language itself.

10. Key Terms
Term

Meaning

Pre-trained model

A model someone else trained on huge amounts of data, shared for reuse.

Fine-tuning

Continuing a pre-trained model’s training on your own labeled examples.

Weights

The millions of numbers inside a model that hold what it learned.

Learning rate

How big each training step is. Fine-tuning uses a small one.

Epoch

One full pass over the training data.

Discriminative model

Sorts inputs into fixed boxes (labels).

Generative model

Creates new text, images, or other content.

Token

The unit a language model predicts: usually a word or a word piece.

Prompting

Steering a model with instructions in the input, without training.
