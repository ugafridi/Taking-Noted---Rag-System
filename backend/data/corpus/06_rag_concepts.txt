Retrieval-Augmented
Generation (RAG)
Week 8, Day 1, Concept Guide

In Week 6 you baked knowledge into a model by fine-tuning it. Today you learn the other way: keep
knowledge outside the model, in documents you control, and hand the model exactly what it needs at
the moment it answers. That method is called Retrieval-Augmented Generation, RAG for short, and it
powers support bots, knowledge assistants, and research tools everywhere.
This is a theory day. There is no code to run and nothing to install. Read carefully, study the figures,
and discuss with your classmates. By the end you can explain what RAG is, how a question becomes
an answer through retrieval, and when to reach for RAG instead of fine-tuning. On Day 2 you build a
working RAG system on Kaggle. On Day 3 you turn it into a full application with a frontend and a
backend.

1. Recap: What You Already Know
In Week 6 you fine-tuned DistilBERT on SMS spam data. You started from a pre-trained
encoder, added a small classification head, and trained with a small learning rate for a few
epochs. Before fine-tuning, accuracy on the test set was 0.5543. After fine-tuning, it was
0.991, with an F1 score of 0.9664. The knowledge you taught it went into the model weights,
and it stays there.
In Week 3 you used the other kind of model: deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3-0324, a generative
model from the Hugging Face Hub. You used it as-is through prompts, with a system prompt,
multi-turn messages, and a context window of 128,000 tokens. You never changed the model
itself.
In Week 5 you learned to judge a model with the confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall,
and F1. That skill returns at the end of this guide, because a RAG system also needs judging,
and judging a generated answer is harder than judging a label.
Today connects all three weeks. Fine-tuning taught a model something permanent. RAG
answers a different question: what if the knowledge changes, or is private, or you have no
labeled pairs to train on?
Key Insight
Fine-tuning puts knowledge into weights. RAG keeps knowledge in documents and hands it
to the model at query time. Same goal, opposite address for the knowledge.

2. The Problem RAG Solves
A language model is frozen in time. Everything it knows comes from its training data, and its
training data ends at a cutoff date. Ask it about anything newer, and it is guessing.
It also knows nothing about your data. Your course notes, a company manual, a clinic guideline,
a shop price list: none of that was in its training data, and for private documents it never should
be.
When a model is asked about something it does not know, it usually answers anyway. It
predicts likely words, not checked facts, so it produces confident answers that are invented.
Practitioners call this hallucination, and it is the central weakness of using a language model
alone for factual questions.
There are two broad solutions. Teach the model, which is fine-tuning, Week 6. Or hand the
model the information at the moment it answers, which is RAG, today. The rest of this guide is
about the second one.
Why Not Just Fine-Tune Everything?
Fine-tuning needs labeled training pairs, GPU time, and a retraining run every time the
knowledge changes. For facts that change monthly, or documents you cannot label by
hand, that price is too high. RAG sidesteps all three.

3. What RAG Is
Retrieval-Augmented Generation means: retrieve relevant information from an external source,
add it to the prompt, and let the model generate an answer grounded in that information.

The name, broken down
•

Retrieval: finding the pieces of your documents that match the question.

•

Augmented: those pieces are added to the prompt, so the prompt carries more than the
question alone.

•

Generation: the language model writes the answer, using the retrieved pieces as its
source.

The core idea is where the knowledge lives. In fine-tuning, knowledge lives inside the model
weights. In RAG, knowledge lives outside the model, in documents you control. The model itself
stays frozen. Nothing is retrained. When a document changes, you edit the document, and the
next answer already reflects the change.
Think of it as an exam. Fine-tuning is a closed-book exam: the student must memorize before
the exam, and anything learned after the exam does not count. RAG is an open-book exam: the
student may look things up, then answer from what they found. Open-book exams test finding
and using information, not memorizing it.
RAG Is a Method, Not a Model
A RAG system uses two models working together: an embedding model that finds relevant
text, and a generative model that writes the answer. You already know both kinds: encoders
from Week 6, and DeepSeek from Week 3.

4. How RAG Works, End to End
A RAG system runs in two phases. The first phase happens once. The second happens every
time someone asks a question.

Phase 1: offline, done once (indexing)
•

Collect the documents: course guides, manuals, notes, policies. This collection is called
the knowledge base.

•

Split each document into small pieces called chunks. Section 7 explains why.

•

Turn every chunk into an embedding, a list of numbers that captures its meaning. Section
5 explains how.

•

Store all the chunk embeddings in a vector store, an index built for similarity search.
Section 6 explains how it is searched.

Indexing is repeated only when the documents change. Add a document, remove one, edit one,
then re-index the changed parts.

Phase 2: online, per question (querying)
•

Embed the question with the same embedding model used for the documents.

•

Search the vector store for the chunks most similar to the question. Take the top few,
usually 3 or 5.

•

Build an augmented prompt: an instruction, the retrieved chunks as context, and the
question.

•

Send that prompt to the generative model. It writes the answer from the context.

•

Optionally, show the sources: which chunks the answer came from, so the reader can
check.

The full RAG pipeline. Indexing happens once, querying happens per question.

One full example
A student asks: "What is fine-tuning?" The question is embedded and searched. The top
chunks come back: one defining fine-tuning, one about small learning rates. The augmented
prompt reads, in skeleton form: "Answer using only the context below. If the context does not
contain the answer, say you do not know. Context: [chunk 1] [chunk 2] Question: What is finetuning?" DeepSeek answers from those chunks, and the answer can point back to them.
The Same-Model Rule
The question and the documents must go through the same embedding model, or their
vectors are not comparable. This is the same rule as Week 6: the tokenizer must match the
model. Mixing models breaks the comparison silently, with no error message.

5. Embeddings
An embedding is a piece of text turned into a list of numbers, called a vector, that captures the
meaning of that text. Two texts with similar meanings get similar vectors. In the language of the
field, they land close together in vector space.
The model that produces embeddings is a transformer encoder, the same family as DistilBERT
from Week 6. DistilBERT reads text and builds a rich representation of it. An embedding model
is an encoder trained so that its representations are good for comparison: similar meanings end
up near each other, different meanings end up far apart.

How many numbers?
That depends on the model. The small, popular model all-MiniLM-L6-v2 produces 384
numbers per text. Larger encoders in the BERT family produce 768 numbers. More numbers
give more room to express meaning, but they also need more storage and more compute. The
numbers below were computed locally for this guide with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, so each sentence
became a 384-number vector.

Semantic search vs keyword search
Keyword search matches exact words. Searching for "car" never finds a document that only
says "automobile". Semantic search matches meaning, because the vectors for "car" and
"automobile" point in a similar direction. That is the whole reason retrieval uses embeddings
instead of word matching.

Real cosine similarity scores from all-MiniLM-L6-v2, rounded to two decimals. Related pairs score clearly higher.

Read the chart carefully. The two related pairs score 0.57 and 0.79, while the unrelated pairs
score 0.03 to 0.09. The gap is the signal retrieval relies on. Also notice that 0.57 is not near 1.0:
the cat sentence and the feline sentence mean similar things but share almost no words, so
their score is moderate. The password pair scores higher partly because the wording overlaps
more. Similarity ranks candidates, it does not grade them like an exam. A score of 0.57 does
not mean "57 percent correct".
What Embeddings Do Not Do
An embedding does not understand text the way a person does. It places similar texts near
each other. That is a much smaller claim, and it is enough for retrieval to work.

Why do car and automobile land close together?
First, a correction: they do not get the same embedding. Same would mean identical numbers,
and that is false. They get similar embeddings: different vectors that point in nearly the same
direction. Measured with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, "a car" vs "an automobile" scored 0.90 and "a car"
vs "a vehicle" scored 0.86, while "a car" vs "a banana" scored 0.37 and "a car" vs "feeling
happy" scored 0.21. Close, not identical. Scores rounded to two decimals.
Why close? Embeddings are learned, not assigned from a dictionary. During training, the model
reads huge amounts of text and learns a simple rule: words that appear in similar contexts tend

to mean similar things. You know a word by the company it keeps. "Car" and "automobile" both
appear near "drive", "road", "engine", "park", and "fuel". Because their contexts overlap so
much, training pushes their vectors toward each other. "Banana" appears near "eat", "fruit",
and "peel", so its vector lands far away.
The same holds for sentences. The model reads the whole sentence, so "I drive my car" and "I
drive my automobile" produce similar sentence vectors because nearly every surrounding word
matches. That is why the cat sentence and the feline sentence in this section scored 0.57:
similar meaning, different words, so close but not identical.

Real positions of word vectors from all-MiniLM-L6-v2, flattened to 2D for display. Car, automobile, and vehicle cluster
together; fruit lands far away.

One caution about this picture: real embeddings have 384 dimensions, and this page has 2.
Flattening 384 dimensions into 2 always distorts some distances. Trust the similarity scores,
not the exact gaps on the page. The scores are measured in 384 dimensions; the picture is
only a sketch of them.

6. Similarity and Retrieval
Cosine similarity
To compare two vectors, RAG systems usually use cosine similarity. It measures the angle
between two vectors, and it runs from -1 to 1. A score near 1 means the vectors point in almost
the same direction, so the meanings are very similar. A score near 0 means they point in
unrelated directions. A negative score means they point in opposite directions, which is rare for
ordinary text. Direction matters here, not length: a long sentence and a short sentence can still
point the same way.

Cosine similarity is about the angle between vectors. Small angle, similar meaning. Large angle, different meaning.

Top-k retrieval
Retrieval returns the k most similar chunks, called the top-k. A common choice is 3 or 5. More
chunks give the model more context, but also more noise, and every extra chunk makes the
prompt longer. Choosing k is a trade-off, and Day 2 lets you feel it by trying different values.

The context window limit
Retrieved chunks must fit inside the generative model context window. DeepSeek, which you
used in Week 3, allows 128,000 tokens. That sounds large, but a library of documents is far
larger, which is exactly why you retrieve the top few chunks instead of sending everything.
Retrieval is a filter that protects the context window.

The vector store
A vector store, also called a vector database or index, is a store built for fast similarity search
over many vectors. FAISS and Chroma are two well known names. For teaching, plain NumPy
search over a small set of vectors works fine and shows the same idea. The job is identical in
every case: given a question vector, return the closest chunk vectors, fast.

Is that all? More ways to search
No. Keyword search and semantic search are the two foundations. Real systems add three
more ideas on top of them.
•

Keyword search (BM25): matches exact words and ranks by a standard formula called
BM25. Rare words count more than common ones, so a match on "fine-tuning" matters
more than a match on "the". Fast and exact, but blind to synonyms: it never connects
"car" with "automobile".

•

Semantic search (dense vectors): matches meaning through embeddings, as in this
guide. Strong on synonyms and phrasing, weak on exact codes: a part number like KX440 has no meaning to match, so vector search can miss it.

•

Hybrid search: runs both methods and combines their rankings into one list, so exact
matches and meaning matches both count. This is the standard production choice, and
Day 2 builds it.

•

Reranking: a second stage, not a search method by itself. After retrieval returns, say, 20
candidates, a second model reads the question together with each chunk and re-scores

them, keeping the best 5. More accurate, more compute, so it only runs on a few
candidates.
•

Metadata filtering: restricting search before or after ranking: only chunks from the 2026
manual, only pages 1 to 10, only the pricing section. Often combined with any method
above.

Hybrid search: one question, two rankings, one combined list.

Day 2 covers hybrid search and reranking in depth. For today, remember the shape: keyword
and semantic are the two foundations, and everything else combines them or refines them.

7. Chunking
Why not embed whole documents? Two reasons. First, token limits: a long document may not
fit into the embedding model input. Second, blurring: one vector for a whole guide mixes many
distinct ideas together, so a question about one section retrieves a vector that is about
everything and nothing in particular.
A chunk is a small piece of a document: a paragraph, or a few hundred characters. Each chunk
gets its own vector, so each vector stands for one idea, and retrieval can return exactly the
idea the question needs.
Chunks usually overlap: neighboring chunks share a little text at the edges, so a sentence that
would otherwise be cut in half survives intact in at least one chunk. The exact chunk size and
overlap are tuning choices, and Day 2 works through them. For today, remember why chunks
exist: small, single-idea pieces retrieve far better than whole documents.

One long document becomes overlapping chunks, each with its own vector.

One sizing rule covers all of them: small chunks retrieve precisely but may lack context, large
chunks carry context but add noise and tokens. Match chunk size to answer size. Short factual
answers suit smaller chunks. Answers needing a whole section suit larger chunks. There is no
single best size, and Day 2 tests this by trying more than one.

When related information lands in different chunks
Suppose a fact you need sits on page 1 and a related fact sits on page 5. After chunking, they
live in two different chunks, far apart in the document. Does that break RAG? No. Retrieval
ranks by similarity to the question, not by position in the document. If both chunks match the
question, both can land in the top-k, even though they are pages apart. The augmented prompt
is simply the retrieved chunks joined together, so the generator sees both and combines them.
That is normal and expected.

Distance in the document does not matter. Two distant chunks can both reach the same prompt.

The real risk is different: the question matches one chunk strongly and the other weakly, so the
second chunk misses the top-k and the answer comes out half complete. Five fixes, from
simplest to strongest:
•

Retrieve more: raise k, so weaker matches still make it into the prompt.

•

Overlap and larger chunks: each chunk carries more surrounding context, so a half-fact is
less likely to be split across a boundary.

•

Parent-document retrieval: search with small chunks for precision, but send the larger
parent, the whole section or page, as context. Small for finding, large for answering.

•

Contextual chunks: prepend the section heading or document title to every chunk before
embedding, so a page 5 chunk still carries its section context and retrieves better.

•

Multi-step retrieval: answer in two rounds. The first retrieval answers part one, then a
follow-up question retrieves part two. Use this when one question truly needs two distant
facts.

Worked example: one question, two distant facts
Take a shop handbook as the document. A customer asks: "I bought a cake yesterday and
want to return it. Is that allowed, and when can I come on Sunday?" Two facts answer it. Page 1
says the shop opens at 9 in the morning on Sundays. Page 5 says cakes can be returned within
2 days of purchase if you bring the receipt. After chunking, those facts live in two different
chunks. Here is how each fix handles it.
•

Retrieve more: suppose with k set to 3, the Sunday hours chunk ranks first, but the
refund chunk ranks fifth, so it misses the prompt and the answer covers only the hours.
Raising k to 5 pulls the refund chunk in, and the answer now covers both parts.

•

Overlap and larger chunks: suppose the refund rule sits at the bottom of page 5, right
where a chunk boundary falls, so "within 2 days of purchase" lands in one chunk and "if
you bring the receipt" lands in the next. With larger chunks and overlap, the whole rule
stays inside a single chunk, and retrieval returns it complete.

•

Parent-document retrieval: the search finds the small chunk "Cakes can be returned
within 2 days of purchase", but the system sends the whole Returns section as context.
That section also says "with a receipt" and "cakes only, not custom orders", so the
answer is complete and does not promise a refund on a custom order.

•

Contextual chunks: each chunk was stored with its section heading prepended, so the
page 5 chunk reads "Section: Returns Policy. Cakes can be returned within 2 days of
purchase if you bring the receipt." The heading adds the word "Returns", which matches
the question word "return", so the chunk ranks higher and makes the top-k on its own
merit.

•

Multi-step retrieval: round one retrieves the Sunday hours chunk and answers that part.
The system then asks a follow-up, "What is the return policy for cakes?", retrieves the
refund chunk, and combines both parts into the final answer.

A related option for structured documents is hierarchical retrieval: retrieve the section summary
first, then drill into that section chunks. Day 2 demonstrates the parent-document and
contextual ideas hands-on.

8. The Augmented Prompt and Grounding
The augmented prompt has three parts: an instruction, the retrieved context, and the question.
A typical template reads:
Prompt Template
"Answer using only the context below. If the context does not contain the answer, say you
do not know. Context: [retrieved chunks] Question: [user question]"
The instruction matters. Without it, the model may answer from its own memory even when the
context says otherwise. With it, the model is told to stay inside the retrieved text. This is called
grounding: the answer is anchored to facts placed in front of the model, instead of facts the
model must recall.
Grounding reduces hallucination, it does not remove it. Two honest limits stay. First, the model
can still misread the context or blend it with its own memory. Second, if retrieval brings the
wrong chunks, the answer will be wrong no matter how good the generator is. Garbage in,
garbage out applies to RAG exactly as it applies everywhere else.
Grounding also buys something fine-tuning cannot: sources. Because the answer came from
specific retrieved chunks, the system can show them. The reader can check the answer
instead of trusting it.

When to use which
•

Use RAG when knowledge changes often, is private, or answers must be checkable
against sources.

•

Use fine-tuning when you need a behavior, a tone, or a skill baked into the model itself. A
support bot that must always speak politely in a fixed format is a fine-tuning job.

•

Use both when you need both. A fine-tuned model for your domain, plus RAG for current
facts, is a common production setup. The two methods are not rivals.

The model roles, tied to your experience
In Week 6 you fine-tuned a classifier, a discriminative model that sorts inputs into labels. RAG
uses a different pair: an embedding model that finds text, and a generative model like
DeepSeek that writes the answer. Different jobs, different models, and now you have touched
all three kinds.

10. Why We Do RAG, and Where It Is Used
Why
•

Private data becomes usable without training on it. Documents stay in your store, not in
model weights.

•

Changing data stays current without retraining. Edit a document and the next answer
reflects it.

•

Answers can cite their sources, so readers can verify instead of trusting.

•

No GPU training and no labeled data. A working RAG system can be built in hours, not
weeks.

•

Transparency: you can inspect exactly which documents an answer came from, which
makes failures debuggable.

Where
•

Support bots answering from product docs and policy pages.

•

Question answering over course material, the Day 2 project.

•

Internal knowledge assistants over company wikis and manuals.

•

Research assistants over papers and reports.

•

Shop chatbots answering from a price list and policy documents, instead of memorizing
prices that change.

11. Limitations
RAG is powerful, and it has sharp edges. Know them before you build.
•

Retrieval fails. It can bring the wrong chunks, miss the right ones, or bring too many,
which drowns the good ones in noise.

•

The model can ignore or misread the context, and answer from memory anyway.

•

RAG does not teach skills or style. A model that writes badly still writes badly, just with
better facts.

•

Chunk quality decides retrieval quality. Bad splits make bad vectors, and bad vectors
make bad retrieval.

•

Longer prompts cost more tokens and more time per question than a plain prompt.

•

Privacy cuts both ways: retrieved documents enter the prompt, so a sensitive document
must never reach the wrong user through retrieval.

•

Evaluation is hard. Week 5 metrics judged a classifier with right or wrong labels. Judging
a generated answer needs new checks: did retrieval find the right chunks, and is the
answer faithful to them? Day 2 introduces those checks.
