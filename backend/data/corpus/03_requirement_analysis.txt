Requirement Analysis
for AI Projects
Week 4, Day 1, Concept Guide

Last week you built a working AI app with nothing written down first. Today you learn what
professionals write down before they build anything: what the system must do, how well it must do it,
and how to ask the people who know. By the end, you can turn a vague idea into requirements
someone could actually test.

1. Why AI Projects Need Requirements
A requirement is a written statement of something a system must do, or a quality it must have.
Requirement analysis is the work of finding out what those statements should be, writing them down
clearly, and getting the people involved to agree on them, before anyone builds anything.
Last week you built an AI app on Hugging Face Spaces with nothing written down first. If someone
had asked how fast it had to be, which kinds of images it had to read, or what it should do with a
blurry photo, you would not have had answers. The model you picked quietly made those decisions
for you. TrOCR, for example, reads one line of text at a time, so an app promising to read a whole
invoice would have failed on its first real test.

Key Insight
Requirements are cheapest to fix on paper. A mistake caught in a conversation costs minutes.
The same mistake caught after the model is trained and deployed can cost weeks.

Running Case: The Invoice Reader
Today uses one made-up case from start to finish. A small accounting office in Peshawar
receives about 200 invoices a week, on paper and as scans. Staff type the details into a
spreadsheet by hand, and mistakes creep in. The owner wants a tool that reads a scanned
invoice and fills in a table. Every example today comes from this case.

2. What Is a Requirement, and What Makes a Good One
Think of a requirement as a promise you can check. If nobody can check whether it was kept, it is not
much of a promise. The difference between a weak requirement and a useful one is usually a
number, or a clear condition.
Vague

Testable

The app should be fast

Returns a result within 3 seconds for 95 percent of requests

It should read invoices well

Reads the total correctly on at least 90 percent of a test set of
100 scanned invoices

It must be secure

Only logged-in staff can open invoices, and invoice images are
deleted after 30 days

It should be easy to use

A new staff member can process an invoice without help after a
10-minute demonstration

The Question to Ask

Clear

Single

Does it say one thing, not two joined by 'and'?

Necessary

Have the main stakeholders accepted it?

Most Common Mistake
Writing the solution instead of the need. 'Use DeepSeek to read invoices' is a solution. 'Read
the total from a scanned invoice' is a need. Naming the tool too early locks the team into it
before anyone knows whether it fits.

4. Elicitation Techniques
Elicitation means drawing requirements out of people. Stakeholders rarely hand them over in a neat
list. They describe problems, habits, and frustrations, and your job is to turn those into requirements.

Interviews
An interview is a planned conversation with one stakeholder about their work. Two styles are
common.
Style

How It Works

Best For

Structured

The same prepared questions are asked to
everyone

Comparing answers across many
people

Open

A conversation that follows what the
person says, with follow-up questions

Finding problems you did not know to
ask about

● Ask about their work, not about the technology.
● Ask why more than once. The first answer is rarely the real one.
● Ask for a recent example instead of a general opinion.
● Write down their exact words, not your summary of them.

Sample Questions for the Invoice Reader
● Walk me through what happens from the moment an invoice arrives.
● Which part of this takes the most time?
● What goes wrong most often?
● Can you show me the worst invoice you have seen?
● What happens if a number is entered wrong?
● Who checks the entries afterward?

Observation
People describe their work as it should happen, not as it does. Sitting beside someone and watching
shows the workarounds, the sticky notes, and the invoices that do not fit the usual format, things no
interview would surface.

5. The Questions Specific to AI
Ordinary software requirements ask what the system does. AI projects need a few more questions,
because an AI system is right most of the time but never all of the time.
Question

Why It Matters

What decision or task is the AI
doing?

'Help with invoices' is not a task. 'Read the supplier, date, and
total' is.

What data exists today?

A model needs examples. With no scanned invoices on file, there
is nothing to test with.

Who labels the data, and how?

Someone must write down the correct answer for each example.

How accurate is good enough?

100 percent is not achievable, so a realistic target has to be
agreed.

What does a wrong answer cost?

A misread total in an accounting record is far worse than a
misread supplier name.

Question

Why It Matters

What happens when the model is
unsure?

The system needs a fallback, such as flagging the value for a
person.

What privacy rules apply?

Invoices contain client names and amounts.

Key Insight
No AI system is right every time. A good requirement document does not promise perfection. It
says how wrong is acceptable, and what happens when the system is wrong.

6. Requirement Templates and the Requirement Document
A template is a ready-made structure that tells you what to ask and where to write the answer. Teams
use them because they remind you of questions you would otherwise forget, they make documents
easy to compare, and they make review faster.

Writing One Requirement Properly
Each requirement gets an ID, one clear statement, a priority, and a way to verify it. The word 'shall' is
a common convention that marks a statement as required, so nobody has to guess which sentences
are mandatory.

Priority

How to Verify

8. Functional Requirements: Input, Output, Workflow
Functional requirements describe what the system does. For an AI system, they are easiest to write
by splitting the system into three parts.
Part

The Questions

Invoice Reader

Input

What goes in, in what format and size,
and from whom?

A scanned invoice as JPG, PNG, or PDF, up to
10 MB, uploaded by staff

Output

What comes out, in what format, and
to whom?

A table of supplier, date, line items, and total,

spreadsheet

Workflow

What steps happen, in what order,
and who does what?

Upload, check the image, read the table, flag
doubtful values, staff review, save

Functional Requirements Specific to AI
Item

Example Requirement

Confidence threshold

The system shall highlight any value with a confidence below 80 percent.

Human review path

A staff member shall be able to correct any value before it is saved.

Unsupported input

The system shall reject other file types and tell the user why.

Logging

The system shall record each upload, result, and correction, so accuracy
can be measured later.

Key Insight
Look at what the workflow includes that a demo would skip: what happens with a bad image,
what happens when the model is unsure, and who is responsible for the final answer. Those are
the parts that decide whether a system is usable in real life.

9. Data Requirements
For an AI project, data is a requirement like any other, and the one most often missing. A model
cannot be built or tested without it.
Data Question

Example Requirement for the Invoice Reader

Source

Scanned invoices from the office's existing files, plus new invoices as they arrive

Format

JPG, PNG, or PDF scans at 200 DPI or higher

Volume

At least 100 invoices for testing, with the correct values written down by hand

Quality

Text readable by a person, with no pages cut off

Labels

A staff member types the correct supplier, date, and total for each test invoice

Privacy

Client names removed or access limited, and a check on whether clients must be
told

Retention

Invoice images deleted after 30 days, extracted values kept as long as
accounting records require

Freshness

New invoice layouts added to the test set every quarter, as suppliers change
their formats

Why AI Projects Often Fail Here
Teams plan the model and discover late that the data is scattered, unlabeled, or not allowed to
be used. Asking the data questions first costs a conversation. Asking them last can end the
project.

11. Functional vs Non-Functional: Telling Them Apart
One quick test sorts almost every requirement. Ask whether it describes something the system does,
or how well it does it. A functional requirement usually contains a verb a user would see, such as
read, show, or export. A non-functional requirement usually contains a number or a quality, such as 3
seconds, secure, or 99 percent.

Functional requirements sit inside the system. Non-functional requirements form the walls around it.

Statement

Type

The system shall let staff correct a value before saving.

Functional

The system shall be available during 99 percent of office hours.

Non-functional (availability)

Invoice images shall be at least 200 DPI.

Data requirement

The system shall flag any value below 80 percent confidence.

Functional

The system shall cost less than Rs 20,000 a month to run.

Non-functional (cost)

At least 100 test invoices shall have hand-written correct values.

Data requirement

Some requirements are hard to classify, and that is fine. Do not spend long on it. The goal is to make
sure all three kinds, functional, non-functional, and data, are written down.
