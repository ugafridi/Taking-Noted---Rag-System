1. From Pieces to a Specification
In Day 1 you learned the parts: how to write a testable requirement, how to tell functional, nonfunctional, and data requirements apart, and how to ask the right questions. This guide puts the parts
together into a specification, a complete document that a developer could build from and a tester
could check against, without asking anyone a single question.
A quick test for any specification is the stranger test. Hand it to someone who was not in the room. If
they can tell what to build, what 'done' means, and what is out of bounds, it passes.

Running Case: The Admissions Document Sorter
This guide uses one made-up case. A university admissions office in Peshawar receives about
3,000 application packets each season. Each packet is a PDF or a set of scans containing a
transcript, an ID card copy, a fee voucher, and a photo. Staff sort every page by hand, which
takes about 6 weeks, and some pages get misfiled or missed. The office wants a system that
labels each page and tells staff which applicants are missing a document. All numbers in this
guide are made up.

When Goals Conflict
The director wants speed, the staff want accuracy, and the legal officer wants documents kept
inside approved systems. Conflicts are normal. Write both goals down, name the trade-off, and
ask the person with the authority to decide. Then record the decision.

4. Problem Statement and Scope
The Problem Statement
A problem statement is two or three sentences that say who has the problem, what it costs, and what
success looks like, with numbers wherever possible.
Fill-in Template
[Who] currently [does what], which causes [problem] costing [measure]. A successful system
will [measurable result].

Example: The Admissions Sorter
Admissions staff currently sort about 3,000 application packets by hand each season, which
takes about 6 weeks and leaves some pages misfiled or missed. A successful system will label
at least 95 percent of pages correctly, send no more than 15 percent of pages to staff for
review, and cut sorting time to under 1 week.

Scope
Scope draws the boundary: what the project will do, and what it will not. The out-of-scope list is the
more useful of the two, because it exposes quiet assumptions.
In Scope

Out of scope does not mean never. It means not in this version.
Key Insight
Scope creep is when new requests quietly pile onto a project until it cannot be finished. An
agreed out-of-scope list gives you a calm answer: that is a good idea, and it belongs in the next
version.

7. Peer Review
You cannot see the gaps in your own writing, because your mind fills them in automatically. A
reviewer's mind cannot, which is exactly why review works.

How a Review Runs
Step

What Happens

The author hands over the specification with a one-line summary of what it is for.

The reviewer reads it once without writing anything, then again with the checklist below.

The reviewer writes comments. Each comment points to a line, says what is wrong, and
suggests a replacement.

The two sides meet. The reviewer explains. The author listens, and asks only: 'what did you
understand?'

The author revises, and notes which comments were accepted and which were not, with a
reason.

10. Edge, Cloud, or Hybrid
Another design choice is where the model runs.

Option

How It Works

Cloud

The model runs in a remote data center. The device sends its data over the internet
and gets the result back.

Edge

The model runs on or beside the device that collects the data, such as a camera
with a small computer attached.

Hybrid

The work is split. Quick, light work happens at the edge, and heavy or long-running
work happens in the cloud.

The same system can be built three ways. The difference is where the model runs.

Tips for Drawing
● Label every arrow with what travels along it.
● Show where people are involved.
● Mark the boundary where data must stay inside approved systems.

Key Insight
A sketch you can explain in one minute is a good sketch. If it takes five minutes, the design is
probably doing too much.

PART C

Tasks: Solve on Paper
These tasks are solved on paper by two teams, Team A and Team B. No laptop is needed. Each team
works together on Tasks 1 to 3, with one person writing and everyone contributing. Where a task
asks for a table, draw it yourselves using the columns named. Your answers do not need to be neat.
They need to be specific. Task 4 is a review between the two teams.
● Both teams solve the same case, so the two sets of answers can be compared.
● All cases are made up. Treat their facts as true, and do not research anything.
● When you write a requirement, use the word 'shall'.
● If your team disagrees, write both views, name the trade-off, and let the team decide.

The Case: HardHat Watch
HardHat Watch is a made-up system for a construction company in Peshawar with 12 building
sites. The company wants cameras at each site's entrance and work areas, and a system that
spots workers who are not wearing a hard hat and alerts the site supervisor right away,
because that worker is at risk at that moment. Several sites have weak mobile internet that
drops often. The company must not store workers' faces. It has about 2,000 site photos taken
by safety officers, none of them labeled, and can spend at most Rs 150,000 a month in total.
TASK 1 OF 4

Start the Spec
What to do
● Write a problem statement for HardHat Watch: who has the problem, what it costs, and what
success looks like. Where the case gives no numbers, invent sensible ones and mark them as
assumptions.
● Write an in-scope list and an out-of-scope list, with at least three items in each.
● Draw a table with the columns Stakeholder, Grid Position, Goal, and Requirement. List at least

four stakeholders. For Grid Position, write manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, or
monitor. For Requirement, write one testable sentence using 'shall'.

Deliverable
A problem statement, two scope lists, and a stakeholder table with at least four rows.

TASK 2 OF 4

Inputs, Outputs, and Vague Words
What to do
● Write an input specification for HardHat Watch with these items: source, format, quality, and
volume.
● Write an output specification with these items: what is produced, who receives it, how fast,

and what is shown with it.

● Write two worked examples, each a real input paired with the exact expected output. At least

one must be an edge case.

● Read the flawed paragraph below. Underline every vague word. Then rewrite it as four

testable requirements using 'shall', each with a number or a clear condition.

The Flawed Paragraph
The system should quickly spot people without proper safety gear in all conditions, and tell the
right people in an easy way. It should be secure and scalable, and work well at night.

Deliverable
Input and output specifications, two worked examples, and four rewritten requirements.
