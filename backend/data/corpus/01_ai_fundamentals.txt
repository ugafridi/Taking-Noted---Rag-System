1. What Is Artificial Intelligence?
Artificial Intelligence is the ability of a machine to perform tasks that normally require human
intelligence, such as recognizing patterns, making decisions, or understanding language. Not every
automated system is AI; a system only counts as AI when it can adapt its output based on data or
experience, rather than following one fixed set of instructions.

2. A Brief History of AI
AI is not new. The field has gone through cycles of hype, disappointment, and breakthrough for over
70 years.
Year

Milestone

Alan Turing proposes the Turing Test to measure machine intelligence

The term Artificial Intelligence is coined at the Dartmouth Conference

1970s to 1980s

The first AI Winter; funding dries up as early promises go unmet

IBM's Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov

A deep learning breakthrough (AlexNet) revives the field using neural

networks
2020s

Generative AI and Large Language Models go mainstream worldwide

The AI Winter was not caused by bad ideas, it was caused by broken promises. Researchers in the
1960s predicted human-level AI within a generation, and when the hardware and data of the time
could not deliver, funding dried up almost overnight.
2012 matters for a reason that often gets skipped. The AlexNet breakthrough was not a smarter
algorithm, it was the same neural network ideas from decades earlier, finally paired with GPU
hardware powerful enough to train them. Old ideas unlocked by new hardware is a pattern worth
remembering whenever you hear bold claims about today's AI wave.

3. Symbolic AI vs Machine Learning AI
There are two fundamentally different ways to build an AI system. Understanding the difference
explains almost everything about why modern AI, including Huawei's platforms, is built the way it is.
Symbolic AI

Machine Learning AI

A programmer writes fixed, hard-coded rules.
Example: for spam detection, a rule might say, if
an email contains the phrase free money, mark it
as spam.

A model is shown thousands of labeled emails
and learns spam patterns on its own, without
anyone writing explicit rules.

Key Insight
Symbolic AI follows rules a human wrote. Machine learning AI finds its own rules from data.
Nearly everything in this course, including Huawei ModelArts, belongs to the machine learning
side.

The stakes get much higher outside of spam filters. Picture a bank deciding loan approvals. A
symbolic system uses a written rule, such as deny if income is below a set threshold, so you can
always point to exactly why someone was rejected. A machine learning system learns the pattern
from thousands of past loans, which can be more accurate, but far harder to explain to a rejected
applicant, or to a regulator asking why. This tradeoff, accuracy versus explainability, comes up
constantly in real AI deployments.

4. Key Domains of AI
AI is not one single technology. It splits into domains, each solving a different kind of problem, and
each domain keeps expanding as the field matures. The map below shows where the field stands
today.

The six domains covered in this course, radiating from the core field of AI.

Computer Vision
Teaches machines to interpret images and video. Two common use cases: security systems using
face detection to grant building access, and manufacturing lines using automated defect inspection
to flag faulty products on a conveyor belt. Maps to Huawei Cloud EI's Image and Video services.

Natural Language Processing (NLP)
Teaches machines to understand and generate human language. Two common use cases: customer
service chatbots that resolve support tickets automatically, and translation tools like Google Translate
converting text between languages in real time. Maps to Huawei Cloud EI's NLP and OCR services.

Robotics
Combines AI with physical machines that sense and act in the real world. Two common use cases:
warehouse robots that pick, sort, and move packages, and autonomous vacuum cleaners that map a
room and avoid obstacles on their own. Maps to Huawei's HiLens edge AI platform, built for exactly
this kind of real-time, on-device inference.

Speech & Audio
Converts spoken language to text and back, and increasingly recognizes sound more broadly. Two
common use cases: voice assistants like Siri or Alexa responding to spoken commands, and call
center software that automatically transcribes and summarizes customer calls. Maps to Huawei
Cloud EI's Speech service.

Generative AI (GenAI)
Creates new content instead of just analyzing existing data. Two common use cases: tools like
Midjourney generating an image from a text prompt, and coding assistants writing code from a plainlanguage description. Huawei's own foundation models in this space are called Pangu.

Agentic AI
Plans and executes multi-step tasks on its own, rather than answering once and stopping. Two
common use cases: an AI agent booking a complete trip, flights, hotel, and transport, from a single
instruction, and a coding agent that writes, tests, and fixes its own code across multiple files without
approval at every step. The newest category, and where Huawei's ModelArts pipelines are headed.

7. Why AI Adoption Is Still a Challenge
Despite the hype, most organizations still struggle to actually deploy AI successfully.
Barrier

What It Means

Data

Many organizations lack clean, labeled data needed to train reliable
models

Regulation

Laws around privacy and AI use are still evolving and vary by country

Cost

Training and running AI models requires expensive infrastructure

Skills gap

Few professionals are trained to build and maintain AI systems

Picture a mid-sized retailer that wants AI-powered demand forecasting. They have years of sales
data, but it is scattered across spreadsheets with no consistent format, the data barrier. Even after
cleaning it, local data protection rules limit what customer data they can use, the regulation barrier.
And the cloud infrastructure needed to train and run the model costs more than their entire IT budget,
the cost barrier. This is why the skills gap matters so much: someone who understands all three
barriers is who actually gets a project like this shipped.

Why This Matters
That last barrier, the skills gap, is exactly the gap this course exists to close. By the end of this
program, you will be one of the trained professionals organizations are struggling to find.

8. The Gartner Hype Cycle: Where Does AI Stand Today?
The Gartner Hype Cycle is a model for how new technologies are typically received over time. It has
five phases, and different AI trends currently sit at different points on the curve.
Phase

What Happens Here

Innovation Trigger

A breakthrough is announced; interest begins but no usable products
exist yet

Peak of Inflated Expectations

Excitement peaks; early success stories generate unrealistic
expectations

Trough of Disillusionment

Interest fades as projects fail to deliver on inflated promises

Slope of Enlightenment

Practical, realistic use cases start to emerge and mature

Plateau of Productivity

The technology becomes a mainstream, reliable part of everyday
tools

As a rough guide, Generative AI currently sits near the Peak of Inflated Expectations, while traditional
machine learning (recommendation engines, fraud detection) has already reached the Plateau of
Productivity.
